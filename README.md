# Development Vocabulary

<details>
<summary>🐈 CAT SHOT</summary>

Inspired by aircraft carrier launch operations.

A carrier deck is one of the most coordinated environments in engineering.

Hundreds of hours of maintenance, training, planning, and preparation all converge into a single moment: launch.

Every role matters. Troubleshooters inspect the system. Pilots verify readiness. The aircraft validates itself through systems checks. Nothing is left informal.

On the deck, motion is precise but chaotic—guided entirely by signals, timing, and discipline.

Then everything narrows.

All planning, briefing, and preparation disappears into a single strip of runway.

Just the jet. Just the launch.

That moment became my software metaphor.

Research → Architecture → Validation → Commit → Launch

A **CAT SHOT commit** means the system has been fully prepared, assumptions have been tested, and execution is no longer theoretical—it is in motion.

</details>

---

<details>
<summary>🚩 RBF</summary>

Originated from a simple but unavoidable moment in systems work:

A system can be fully built, fully tested, and still not be ready.

There is always a gap between what we think we built and what actually exists.

RBF was created for that gap.

It is the final pass before release where the system is treated as if it is already in production. Not to test functionality, but to confront reality.

In that moment, everything unnecessary becomes visible. Assumptions surface. Edge cases reappear. Intent is rechecked against implementation.

Nothing new is added.

Only what does not belong is removed.

That final moment is the difference between a system that works in theory and a system that survives contact.

Remove Before Flight.

</details>

---

<details>
<summary>🛰 NASA-10</summary>

Inspired by NASA’s approach to software and engineering in mission-critical systems.

In environments where failure is not an option, code is not judged by how elegant or compact it is—but by how clearly it can be understood under pressure, by someone who did not write it.

NASA-10 is my term for a deliberate refactor that restores that clarity.

It is not about reducing lines of code.

It is about reducing uncertainty.

It is the moment a system stops optimizing for expression and starts optimizing for comprehension—where structure, intent, and behavior become easier to reason about than to misinterpret.

Readable systems are not a preference.

They are a requirement for systems that are expected to last.

</details>

---

<details>
<summary>🚀 YF-Twelved</summary>

Inspired by the YF-12 interceptor program.

A machine built for speed at the edge of what was physically controllable.

It could move faster than the systems designed to support it could comfortably operate.

Detection, tracking, decision-making, and engagement all lived in a narrower and more fragile window than the platform itself could sustain.

On paper, it was extraordinary.

In practice, capability began to outrun usability.

That gap became the lesson.

Systems do not fail only from lack of power.

They fail when their surrounding structure cannot translate power into action.

When observability lags behind execution.  
When deployment cannot keep pace with change.  
When complexity exceeds what can be safely operated.

If “YF-Twelved” appears in my commit history, it implies a moment where I scaled the system before its surrounding architecture was fully ready to support it.

</details>

---

<details>
<summary>🧯 PPL2PP — Poor Planning Leads 2 Poor Performance</summary>

Inspired by recurring failure patterns in high-stress system design.

In complex systems, breakdowns rarely begin at the moment of failure.

They begin much earlier—quietly—during planning.

A constraint is missed.  
A dependency is assumed.  
A boundary is left undefined.  
A shortcut is labeled “temporary.”

Under normal load, nothing breaks.

But high-stress systems do not fail gradually.

They fail suddenly, at the edges of what was never designed.

PPL2PP describes this cascade.

Poor planning does not just reduce performance.

It moves failure closer to reality.

In high-stress architecture—where latency, concurrency, scale, and reliability all converge—every assumption becomes a pressure point.

When those assumptions are not surfaced early, they do not disappear.

They accumulate.

Until the system is no longer being stressed by load—

but by its own design decisions.

PPL2PP is a reminder that performance is not the first problem.

Planning is.

</details>

---

<details>
<summary>📚 TOC — Table of Contents</summary>

Inspired by the need to make systems navigable under pressure.

A repository is never just code.

It is structure, intent, decisions, and history layered together over time.

Without a map, even well-written systems become difficult to enter.

A Table of Contents exists to remove that friction.

It is the first point of orientation.

The first reduction of uncertainty.

The place where a system stops being a collection of files and becomes something understandable.

Every project gets a TOC because understanding should not depend on memory.

It should be visible from the start.

</details>
